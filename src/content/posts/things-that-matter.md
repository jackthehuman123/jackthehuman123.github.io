I've started to realize that software engineering in this generation now focuses on 3 main pillars.

## Leverage (own the stack)

Agentic coding is here to stay. As much as I was an anti of AI coding, I've come to the realization that software development has "evolved" into running agents, harnesses and evals well enough to do the work of a small team.

It is an engineer's instinct to have the urge to control every line of output coming from "letters predictors" that have no sense of reasoning. "What if the syntax fails?", "What if this logic is implemented incorrectly?". The truth is, leveraging agentic workflows produces an amount of outcomes that cannot be fully managed by humans, and we need to accept that.

Accepting means creating autonomous measures such as evals, testing, etc. — practices that have been used in software engineering from time to time, with a good balance of human intervention.

## Depth (own the knowledge)

One may ask, "If AI acts as a Wikipedia / source of truth, what's the point of knowing things in depth, when knowledge is one prompt away?"

No one can ensure that within the next few years, LLMs won't be able to take care of writing eval loops and carry out testing for all parts of an application. In fact, we can just tell the agents to run a bunch of scripts covering all edge cases and branches, log it out, write tests, reimplement code, loop it, and we'll get the desired results.

So, "we" "tell the agents" will be my current answer to this section. It is true that one day, an agent will be capable enough to write all sorts of tests they can come up with. But with domain knowledge and technical depth, we humans can verify whether all those test cases are enough, and carry out more iterations.

## Judgment (own the direction)

Let's be honest, everyone wants to make money. From a businessman's perspective, what's more fascinating than an autonomous machine that pumps out half-assed ideas while generating good cash flow?

Slop wins on volume. A clever person understands that the more you make, the more the odds tilt in your favor. Judgment wins on quality, retention and brand image.

But here's the catch: when agents make slop free for everyone, volume stops being an edge. Everyone can pump out a hundred apps a week, so a hundred apps a week becomes the baseline. What's left scarce is the thing that's actually well-made, and scarce things win.

That doesn't mean volume is wrong. The best builders use it on purpose: throw out ten cheap prototypes, see what sticks, then pour real care into the one that matters. That's the hybrid, and it's judgment in action — knowing what to build, what to cut, where to go fast, where to go deep, and when it's done. Slop is just volume with no one making those calls.

Agents can build almost anything now. Deciding what's worth building is still on us.
